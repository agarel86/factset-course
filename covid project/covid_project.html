Project

Which French listed firms lost the most during the COVID-19 crash, and why?

A small research project from the question to the conclusion, done entirely with FactSet: the data come from FactSet 365 formulas in Excel and every analysis is an Excel formula. Each step is one sheet of the workbook. Try each step first; open the hint if you are stuck and the solution once you have a result.

1

Research question

The COVID-19 pandemic was a large and unexpected shock. Between 20 February and 16 March 2020 the French stock market lost more than a third of its value. Investors revised their expectations of firms' future cash flows and risk, but not by the same amount for every firm.

Question: which characteristics of French listed firms, measured before the crisis, explain their stock-market performance during the COVID-19 crash?

Unit of observation: the firm. Universe: the members of the CAC All-Tradable index on 20 February 2020, the first day of the window. Event window: 20 February – 16 March 2020. Characteristics: fiscal year 2019.

2

Arguments and hypotheses

If investors expect lower future profits or a higher risk of distress, prices fall more; if they expect stable or higher demand, prices fall less.

HypothesisArgumentExpected sign
H1 Exposed industries fell moreLockdowns and travel bans stop transport, energy demand, tourism, restaurants and leisure.Exposed −
H2 Defensive industries fell lessHealth care, pharmaceuticals and consumer staples face stable or higher demand.Defensive +
H3 Financially constrained firms fell moreWith a revenue stop, high debt and little cash raise the risk of a liquidity shortfall and distress.Leverage −, Cash +
H4 Profitable firms fell lessPre-crisis profitability signals margins that can absorb a shock and easier access to financing.Profitability +
H5 Firms with better ESG ratings fell lessESG quality may reflect better risk management and more loyal investors (the "resilience" argument).ESG +

Retail is treated with care: firms with physical stores and firms that sell online were affected differently, so retail is neither "exposed" nor "defensive".

4

The regression

CovidReturni = α + β1 Leveragei + β2 Cashi + β3 Profitabilityi + β4 Sizei + γ1 Exposedi + γ2 Defensivei (+ δ ESGi) + εi

CovidReturni: total return of firm i from 20 February to 16 March 2020 (%). Exposed / Defensive: dummies equal to 1 for the firms of the exposed / defensive sectors. Estimated by OLS (Excel LINEST) in four specifications: (1) firm characteristics, (2) industry dummies, (3) both, (4) both plus ESG on the firms with an ESG rating.

Your turn: run the project on your own

Before opening the steps below, build the project yourself in Excel with FactSet 365, from the list of firms to the regressions. Think about each point.

  1. Universe. Which index covers the largest set of French listed firms? Find its FactSet identifier and get, with one formula, its constituents at the start of the crisis rather than today. Why does the date of the list matter?
  2. Performance. Which measure of performance, over which window? Total return or price change? How do you write the dates in the FactSet request?
  3. Firm characteristics. Which items measure leverage, cash, profitability, size and ESG? For which fiscal year and which date, so that they are known before the crisis? In which unit and currency?
  4. Industry. How do you classify firms as exposed or defensive with the FactSet sector? Which sectors go in each group, and which do you leave out?
  5. Sample. Which firms have all the variables? What do you do with missing values? Should banks and insurers stay in a study that uses debt over assets? How many firms have an ESG rating?
  6. Analysis. Descriptive statistics and correlations; differences in mean return between groups (t-tests); OLS regressions with several specifications.
  7. Conclusion. Which hypotheses are supported, at which significance level, and what are the limits of your analysis?

Then compare your work with the steps and their solutions below.

Step 1 · Constituents of the CAC All-Tradable on 20 February 2020

The step asks for: one row per member of the CAC All-Tradable index on 20 February 2020, with the company name and its FactSet sector (sheet 1_Constituents).

Your turn

Where to look
Excel, FactSet 365 › Insert Formula: search constituents, then company name and sector.
Pay attention to
  • The list comes from one formula on the index identifier: no download from the platform.
  • Ask for the members on the first day of the window, not today's members. Today's list misses the firms that left the index or were delisted after 2020, and it includes firms that joined later; a study of 2020 built on it would look only at the survivors.
Expected result
Close to three hundred firms spill down, each with a name and a sector. That is more than in today's index, because many firms have left it since 2020.
Hint

The item Benchmark Constituents (FG_CONSTITUENTS) reads the index in the identifier cell; the CAC All-Tradable is CACT-ENX. Without an argument it returns today's members. With a date in brackets, FG_CONSTITUENTS(02/20/2020), it returns the members on that date.

Solution

The date in B4 is written MM/DD/YYYY, as in every FactSet request. A6 spills one identifier (SEDOL) per member, and B6:C6 are copied down. The solution workbook lists 294 firms on 20 February 2020.

The FTSE Russell ESG rating. From 0 to 5; 5 is the best. It is built from the firm's practices on environmental, social and governance themes (climate change, pollution, labour standards, human rights, anti-corruption, risk management…), each theme score weighted by how exposed the firm is to it given its business and countries. "ABS" asks for the absolute rating, comparable across industries; a relative rating compares a firm with the others of its industry. A missing value means FactSet has no FTSE Russell rating for the firm, not a rating of 0.

What the t-tests say about the hypotheses

Each t-test compares two groups of firms as they are, without holding anything else constant. Group 1 is the exposed, defensive or above-median group; the difference is group 1 minus group 2, in percentage points of return.

  • H1: exposed firms lost about 2 points more than the others, which is the expected sign, but the difference is not significant (p = 0.44). The exposed group is small, with about twenty firms.
  • H2: defensive firms lost about 5 points less than the others, and the difference is significant at 5% (p = 0.026). This is the clearest result of the t-tests.
  • H3a: the more indebted half of the sample lost about 1.5 points more than the less indebted half, which is the expected sign, but the difference is not significant (p = 0.35).
  • H3b: the half with more cash lost about 3 points more than the other half. This is the opposite of H3b, and the difference is significant at 10% only (p = 0.060).
  • H4: the more profitable half lost about 1.8 points less than the less profitable half, which is the expected sign, but the difference is not significant (p = 0.26).
  • H5: among the rated firms, those above the median ESG rating did no better than those below it (p = 0.97).

A split at the median hides what happens in the tails. The effect of leverage and profitability comes mostly from the firms at the extremes, the most indebted and the loss-making ones, and comparing two halves dilutes them among many average firms. The regressions of step 8 use the actual values of each variable and hold the others constant.

What the regressions say about the hypotheses

H1 to H4 are read in specification 3 (all firms, industry and financial characteristics together, R² 0.110); H5 in specification 4 (the 69 firms with an ESG rating).

  • H1, exposed sectors fell more: Exposed −1.81 (p = 0.504). The sign is the expected one, but the effect is small and not significant, so H1 is not supported. Only a few firms are in the exposed group, and the FactSet sector Consumer Services mixes hotels and restaurants with media and publishing firms, which were much less affected.
  • H2, defensive sectors fell less: Defensive +9.05 (p < 0.001). The sign is the expected one and the effect is significant at 1%, so H2 is supported: with the same financial characteristics, defensive firms lost about 9 points less than the others.
  • H3a, high leverage, worse return: Leverage −9.38 (p = 0.027). The sign is the expected one and the effect is significant at 5%, so H3a is supported: a firm with 10 points more debt over assets lost almost 1 point more.
  • H3b, high cash, better return: Cash −7.78 (p = 0.181). The sign is the opposite of H3b, but the effect is not significant, so H3b is not supported. The firms with the most cash are mostly loss-making biotech firms (cash and profitability have a correlation of −0.49); once profitability is in the model, cash adds little information of its own.
  • H4, profitable firms fell less: Profitability +15.42 (p < 0.001). The sign is the expected one and the effect is significant at 1%, so H4 is supported: 10 more points of EBITDA over assets go with a return about 1.5 points better.
  • H5, better ESG rating, better return: ESG +1.37 (p = 0.501) in specification 4. The effect is not significant, so H5 is not supported.

Why the date of the list matters. With today's members of the index instead of the members of February 2020, leverage and profitability were not significant and the cash coefficient was significantly negative. The firms that have left the index since 2020 are a large part of the sample, and they are smaller and more often loss-making than the others. Leaving them out removes precisely the firms where leverage and profitability made a difference, and changes the conclusions on H3 and H4.

Conclusion of the solution

  • Industry: H2 is supported, H1 is not. Defensive firms (health, consumer staples) lost about 9 points less than the others with the same financial characteristics, significant at 1%; the t-test gives the same picture. Exposed firms did not fall significantly more, in the t-test or in the regression. The exposed group is small, and the FactSet sector Consumer Services also contains media and publishing firms, which dilutes the effect of hotels, restaurants and leisure.
  • Financial constraints: H3a is supported, H3b is not. Holding profitability, size and industry constant, the more indebted firms lost more (significant at 5%), as expected when revenue stops and debt must still be served. Cash has the sign opposite to H3b but is not significant: the firms with the most cash are mostly loss-making biotech firms, and their cash says more about their business model than about their resilience.
  • Profitability: H4 is supported, although the t-test did not show it. The two tools answer different questions. The t-test compares the more and the less profitable halves of the sample as they are, and finds a small difference that is not significant. The regression finds a strong effect for two reasons. First, it uses the actual values: the less profitable half is mostly made of profitable firms, and the loss-making firms, which lost about 41% against 37% for the others, are diluted among them. Second, it holds industry constant: about half of the defensive firms are loss-making biotech or medtech firms, and the good performance of the defensive group as a whole hides their losses in a simple comparison. Once the Defensive dummy captures the industry bonus, the effect of profitability appears clearly.
  • ESG: H5 is not supported. The ESG rating has no effect on the firms it covers, neither in the t-test nor in specification 4.
  • The date of the list changes the conclusions. With today's members of the index, leverage and profitability were not significant. The firms that have left the index since 2020 are smaller and more often loss-making; dropping them biases the sample towards survivors and hides the role of financial health in the crash.
  • Fit. The R² stays low, about 0.11 in specification 3. Most of the crash was common to all firms: all firms but three fell, and the median firm lost about 39%. The characteristics in the model explain only a small part of the differences between firms; the rest comes from factors that the model does not capture.
  • Limits. Several limits call for caution when reading these results.
    • The event window is short: it covers less than a month, from 20 February to 16 March 2020. It captures the panic of the crash, not the recovery that followed, and a firm that fell sharply may have recovered quickly afterwards.
    • The FactSet sector is only a rough measure of a firm's exposure to the pandemic. All firms of a sector receive the same dummy, although they can be affected very differently: Health Technology mixes pharmaceutical groups and loss-making biotech firms, and Consumer Services mixes hotels and restaurants with media.
    • Banks and insurers are excluded because debt over assets does not measure the same thing for them. The results therefore say nothing about the financial sector.
    • The FTSE Russell ESG rating covers only about a quarter of the sample, mostly large firms. The test of H5 rests on a small group of firms that is not representative of the whole index.
    • The regressions measure correlations, not causal effects. A coefficient tells us that firms with a given characteristic did better or worse on average, not that this characteristic caused the difference: another variable that is not in the model may explain both.
